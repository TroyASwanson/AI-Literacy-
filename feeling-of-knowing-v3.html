Question 01 — Reveal
Your response
Answer
On average, all highly intelligent people marry less intelligent partners. It's just math — regression to the mean. Gender has nothing to do with it.
What to notice
This question was framed to make you think about gender, but gender has nothing to do with the answer. Notice how the framing shaped your response — your mind likely started building explanations about why women specifically would do this. That's your schemas at work. Schemas are bundles of memories, experiences, and assumptions that activate automatically when you encounter a topic. The word "women" activated a whole set of associations that pulled your thinking in a particular direction. You weren't reasoning from evidence — you were constructing a story based on patterns your mind already had. This is one way the feeling of knowing can mislead us: the question felt like it had a social or psychological answer because of how it was framed, not because of the actual math behind it.
Question 02 / 04
Who was the 8th president of the United States?
How confident are you in your answer?
Just guessingCompletely sure
Question 02 — Reveal
Your response
Answer
Martin Van Buren was the eighth President of the United States (1837–1841), after serving as the eighth Vice President and the tenth Secretary of State under President Andrew Jackson.
What to notice
Pay attention to what your confidence rating felt like. If you were unsure, you probably experienced that uncertainty as a feeling — a kind of blankness or hesitation. If you guessed confidently and got it wrong, that's even more interesting. The feeling of knowing isn't based on whether you actually have the right information stored in memory. It's based on things like familiarity (does a name come to mind quickly?), fluency (does an answer feel smooth and easy?), and associations (do you have memories connected to this topic?). You can feel certain without being correct, and feel uncertain while actually having the right answer. The feeling and the knowledge are two separate things, even though your mind presents them as one.

What to notice
If you said $0.10, you experienced something psychologists call a fluency effect — the answer came quickly, felt effortless, and seemed obviously right. That ease is a signal your mind uses to judge accuracy: if it comes to mind easily, it must be correct. But fluency isn't the same as accuracy. Your mind's goal is efficiency — it wants to move from conscious effort to automatic processing as fast as possible. That's useful for survival, but it means your first instinct often prioritizes speed over precision. The feeling of knowing was strong here precisely because the wrong answer felt so natural. This is why the feeling of knowing can be especially unreliable on questions that seem simple.

What to notice
Unlike the first three questions, this one has no factual answer to check against — but the feeling of knowing is still operating. You probably picked a number without fully articulating why. That's because your answer emerged from your schemas — your accumulated experiences, values, and assumptions about how the economy works, who deserves what, and what fairness means. These schemas run in the background and produce a feeling of "rightness" about your answer. Notice too whether you had an emotional reaction: did the question make you feel frustrated, passionate, or defensive? Emotion is a key ingredient in the feeling of knowing. When a topic connects to your identity or values, the feeling of knowing gets stronger — but that strength comes from personal investment, not from evidence. This is especially important to recognize when you encounter information sources on topics you care about.

Two Things Come Together When You "Know" Something

1
The Memory
The thing that is known — the actual information stored in your mind
2
The Feeling
A judgment your mind makes about that memory — shaped by familiarity, fluency, emotion, and identity

Understanding this feeling is the foundation of everything else in this course, because every time you encounter an information source — a news article, a scholarly paper, an AI-generated response — your mind is making these judgments before you've consciously evaluated anything.

Learning to notice the feeling of knowing is the first step toward thinking critically about what you read, hear, and believe.
